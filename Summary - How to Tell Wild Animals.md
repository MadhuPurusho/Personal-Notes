### 1. Overview & Theme

**Poet:** Carolyn Wells  
**Genre:** Humorous Poetry / Satire  
**Rhyme Scheme:** `ababcc` (Uniform across all stanzas)  

> **Core Theme:** The poem offers humorous and absurd rules to identify dangerous wild animals. The poet suggests that you can recognize these animals based on how they attack or kill you—creating a witty contrast between practical identification and fatal danger.

---

### 2. Animal Identification Guide (According to the Poem)

| Animal | Distinctive Traits / Appearance | How to Identify (Humorous Attack Method) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Asian Lion** | Large, dull yellowish-brown ("tawny") beast. | It roars at you so terrifyingly that you die of fear. |
| **Bengal Tiger** | Noble beast with black stripes on a yellow coat. | You'll know it's a Bengal Tiger if it simply eats you. |
| **Leopard** | Hide peppered with dark spots. | It leaps on you repeatedly, ignored by your cries of pain ("lep and lep again"). |
| **Bear** | Large creature found in your yard. | Gives you a tight, crushing "hug" (fatal embrace). |
| **Hyena** | Scavenger with a smiling face. | It appears to be grinning/laughing merrily while eating its prey. |
| **Crocodile** | Reptile residing in water bodies. | Sheds false tears while swallowing its victim ("Crocodile tears"). |
| **Chameleon** | Small lizard-like reptile with no ears or wings. | Changes color to camouflage; if the tree looks empty, it's there. |

---

### 3. Key Literary & Poetic Devices

* **Poetic License:** The poet intentionally changes word spellings to maintain the rhyme scheme and rhythm:
  * *"lept"* and *"lep"* instead of *leaped* and *leap* to rhyme with *leopard*.
  * *"dyin'"* instead of *dying* to rhyme with *lion*.
* **Irony:** Presenting a life-threatening situation (getting eaten or mauled) as a simple, informative "rule of learning".
* **Hyperbole (Exaggeration):** Exaggerated descriptions, such as dying purely from a lion's roar or confirming a bear by letting it hug you again.
* **Alliteration:** Repetition of initial consonant sounds:
  * *"roaming round"*
  * *"he'll only lep and lep again"*
  * *"A novice might nonplus"*
* **Paradox:** *"If there is nothing on the tree, / 'Tis the chameleon you see."* (How can you see something if nothing is there?)

### 4. Textual & Board-Style Questions

#### Q1. How does the poet suggest you identify an Asian Lion and a Bengal Tiger?
**Answer:** The poet states that if an animal with a yellowish-brown coat roars so loudly that you feel like you are dying of fear, it is an Asian Lion. On the other hand, if a majestic wild beast with black stripes on a yellow coat greets you and starts eating you, it is a Bengal Tiger.

#### Q2. What is the significance of the phrase "bear hug" in the poem?
**Answer:** In reality, a "bear hug" is a warm, tight hug given out of affection. However, the poet humorously uses it to describe a real bear's deadly physical grip that crushes its victim to death.

#### Q3. How can a novice distinguish between a Hyena and a Crocodile?
**Answer:** A novice can distinguish them by their facial expressions while hunting: a Hyena always approaches with a merry smile, whereas a Crocodile sheds tears while consuming its prey.

#### Q4. Why does the poet say that if there is nothing on the tree, the chameleon is there?
**Answer:** The chameleon is a lizard-like reptile capable of changing its skin color to match its surroundings (camouflage). Because it blends in perfectly, it becomes invisible to the naked eye; hence, even if a branch looks empty, a chameleon might be sitting on it.

### **Stanza 1: The Asian Lion**

> _If ever you should go by chance_
> 
> _To jungles in the east;_
> 
> _And if there should to you advance_
> 
> _A large and tawny beast,_
> 
> _If he roars at you as you’re dyin’_
> 
> _You’ll know it is the Asian Lion…_

- **Explanation:** The poet says if you ever travel to the forests in the East (Asia) and encounter a large, yellowish-brown (_tawny_) animal coming toward you, you can identify it if it roars so fiercely that you feel like you are dying of fear.
    
- **Humor:** The poet suggests identifying the lion only as you are about to die from its roar.
    
- **Poetic Liberty:** The poet uses **"dyin'"** instead of "dying" to maintain the rhyme scheme with "lion".
    

### **Stanza 2: The Bengal Tiger**

> _Or if some time when roaming round,_
> 
> _A noble wild beast greets you,_
> 
> _With black stripes on a yellow ground,_
> 
> _Just notice if he eats you._
> 
> _This simple rule may help you learn_
> 
> _The Bengal Tiger to discern._

- **Explanation:** If you are wandering around the jungle and meet a grand, regal-looking (_noble_) wild beast with a yellow coat covered in black stripes, you will know it is a Bengal Tiger if it attacks and eats you.
    
- **Humor & Irony:** Calling a wild animal "noble" and saying it "greets" you, followed by the absurd advice that being eaten is the way to learn its identity.
    

### **Stanza 3: The Leopard**

> _If strolling forth, a beast you view,_
> 
> _Whose hide with spots is peppered,_
> 
> _As soon as he has lept on you,_
> 
> _You’ll know it is the Leopard._
> 
> _’Twill do no good to roar with pain,_
> 
> _He’ll only lep and lep again._

- **Explanation:** While walking forward casually in the forest, if you see an animal whose skin (_hide_) is covered (_peppered_) with dark spots, it is a Leopard. The moment it spots you, it will leap on you. Crying out in pain will not help because it will only keep pouncing on you continuously.
    
- **Poetic Liberty:** The words **"lept"** and **"lep"** are misspelled intentionally to rhyme with "leopard".
    

### **Stanza 4: The Bear**

> _If when you’re walking round your yard_
> 
> _You meet a creature there,_
> 
> _Who hugs you very, very hard,_
> 
> _Be sure it is a Bear._
> 
> _If you have any doubts, I guess_
> 
> _He’ll give you just one more caress._

- **Explanation:** If you are walking around your backyard and meet a creature that wraps its arms around you in a tight embrace, it is a Bear. If you still doubt its identity, it will give you one more tight grip (_caress_).
    
- **Humor & Irony:** A bear's "hug" is actually its deadly attack where it crushes its prey to death, but the poet describes it as a warm, affectionate hug/caress.
    

### **Stanza 5: Hyena and Crocodile**

> _Though to distinguish beasts of prey_
> 
> _A novice might nonplus,_
> 
> _The Crocodile you always may_
> 
> _Tell from the Hyena thus:_
> 
> _Hyenas come with merry smiles;_
> 
> _But if they weep they’re Crocodiles._

- **Explanation:** A beginner or inexperienced person (_novice_) might find it confusing (_nonplus_) to differentiate between wild predators. However, the poet offers a simple rule: Hyenas always appear to be smiling merrily when hunting, while Crocodiles shed fake tears while swallowing their victims.
    
- **Idioms/Metaphors:** References popular animal idioms—the laughing hyena and "crocodile tears" (fake sorrow).
    

### **Stanza 6: The Chameleon**

> _The true Chameleon is small,_
> 
> _A lizard sort of thing;_
> 
> _He hasn’t any ears at all,_
> 
> _And not a single wing._
> 
> _If there is nothing on the tree,_
> 
> _’Tis the chameleon you see._

- **Explanation:** A true chameleon is a small, lizard-like reptile with no ears or wings. It has the ability to change its skin color to match its surroundings (camouflage). Therefore, if you look at a tree branch and see nothing at all, it means a chameleon is sitting there blending in.
    

### **Key Poetic Devices Across All Stanzas**

1. **Rhyme Scheme:** Uniform `ababcc` throughout all 6 stanzas (e.g., _chance-advance, east-beast, dyin'-lion_).
    
2. **Poetic License:** Intentionally altering language/spellings for rhythm and humor (_dyin'_, _lept_, _lep_).
    
3. **Irony & Black Humor:** Using dangerous, life-threatening animal attacks as "learning guides".
    
4. **Alliteration:** Repetition of consonant sounds:
    
    - _"roaming round"_
        
    - _"novice might nonplus"_
        
    - _"he'll only lep and lep again"_
