### 1. Overview & Theme

**Poet:** Robin Klein  
**Genre:** Child Psychology / Reflective Poetry  
**Rhyme Scheme:** 
* Stanzas with nagging (`aaba` or `aaaa` pattern): *control / Amanda / straight / Amanda*
* Stanzas in parentheses (Amanda's fantasy worlds, `aaa` pattern): *sea / me*, *street / feet*, *tower / power*

> **Core Theme:** The desire for freedom and space in childhood. The poem highlights the conflict between parental nagging/constant instructions and a young child's inner world of imagination and need for independence.

---

### 2. Stanza-by-Stanza Explanation

#### Stanza 1: The Nagging Begins
> *Don’t bite your nails, Amanda!*  
> *Don’t hunch your shoulders, Amanda!*  
> *Stop that slouching and sit up straight, Amanda!*

* **Speaker:** Parent / Guardian (likely mother)
* **Explanation:** The parent gives a series of instructions to correct Amanda's posture and habits—telling her not to bite her nails, not to hunch her shoulders, and to sit up straight.

---

#### Stanza 2: Amanda’s First Fantasy (The Mermaid)
> *(There is a languid, emerald sea,*  
> *where the sole inhabitant is me—*  
> *a mermaid, drifting blissfully.)*

* **Speaker:** Amanda (Inner thoughts in parentheses)
* **Explanation:** To escape the constant instructions, Amanda retreats into her imagination. She pictures herself as a solitary **mermaid** swimming peacefully in a calm (*languid*), green (*emerald*) ocean, completely free and unbothered.

---

#### Stanza 3: Daily Routine Instructions
> *Did you finish your homework, Amanda?*  
> *Did you tidy your room, Amanda?*  
> *I thought I told you to clean your shoes, Amanda!*

* **Speaker:** Parent / Guardian
* **Explanation:** The parent fires another round of questions regarding chores and responsibilities—asking about her homework, her room, and her shoes.

---

#### Stanza 4: Amanda’s Second Fantasy (The Orphan)
> *(I am an orphan, roaming the street.*  
> *I pattern soft dust with my hushed, bare feet.*  
> *The silence is golden, the freedom is sweet.)*

* **Speaker:** Amanda
* **Explanation:** Feeling suffocated by instructions, Amanda imagines being an **orphan** wandering the streets barefoot. In her eyes, being an orphan would mean absolute quiet (*silence is golden*) and total independence (*freedom is sweet*) with no one to constantly control her.

---

#### Stanza 5: Personal Grooming & Restrictions
> *Don’t eat that chocolate, Amanda!*  
> *Remember your acne, Amanda!*  
> *Will you please look at me when I’m speaking to you, Amanda!*

* **Speaker:** Parent / Guardian
* **Explanation:** The parent forbids her from eating chocolate due to skin concerns (*acne*) and reprimands her for not paying direct attention when being spoken to.

---

#### Stanza 6: Amanda’s Third Fantasy (Rapunzel)
> *(I am Rapunzel, I have not a care;*  
> *life in a tower is tranquil and rare;*  
> *I’ll certainly never let down my bright hair!)*

* **Speaker:** Amanda
* **Explanation:** Amanda envisions herself as **Rapunzel**, living alone high in a tranquil tower far away from people. Unlike the fairytale Rapunzel, Amanda decides she will *never* let her hair down for anyone, as she does not want her quiet life interrupted.

---

#### Stanza 7: The Final Warning
> *Stop that sulking at once, Amanda!*  
> *You’re always so moody, Amanda!*  
> *Anyone would think that I nagged at you, Amanda!*

* **Speaker:** Parent / Guardian
* **Explanation:** The parent notices Amanda's withdrawn behavior and accuses her of being moody and sulking. The irony peaks in the final line where the parent worries about public perception (*"anyone would think that I nagged at you"*), despite having nagged her throughout the poem.

---

### 3. Key Literary Devices

* **Parentheses (Brackets):** Used to separate Amanda's inner imaginary world from the external world of parental instructions.
* **Anaphora:** Repetition of a word/phrase at the start of consecutive lines:
  * *"Don't bite..."* / *"Don't hunch..."*
  * *"Did you finish..."* / *"Did you tidy..."*
* **Alliteration:** Repetition of consonant sounds:
  * *"Stop that **s**louching and **s**it up **s**traight"*
  * *"I thought I told you to **c**lean your **s**hoes"*
* **Metaphor:** 
  * *"emerald sea"* (comparing green ocean water to an emerald gemstone)
  * *"silence is golden, the freedom is sweet"*
* **Allusion:** Direct reference to famous figures/stories:
  * **Mermaid** (Mythological creature representing peaceful freedom)
  * **Rapunzel** (Fairytale character living in an isolated tower)
* **Repetition:** The name **"Amanda!"** is repeated at the end of almost every instruction to emphasize continuous nagging.

### 4. Textual & Board-Style Questions

#### Q1. How old do you think Amanda is? How do you know this?
**Answer:** Amanda is likely a young school-going teenager (around 10 to 13 years old). We can infer this from the nature of the instructions she receives—doing homework, cleaning her room, being warned about acne from eating chocolate, and being told not to bite her nails.

#### Q2. Why are stanzas 2, 4, and 6 given in parentheses?
**Answer:** The stanzas in parentheses represent Amanda’s internal monologue and imaginative daydreams. They provide a sharp contrast to the outer stanzas where her parent is constantly nagging her. The brackets visually separate her free inner world from the controlled external world.

#### Q3. What does Amanda mean by "silence is golden, the freedom is sweet"?
**Answer:** Amanda feels overwhelmed by the constant noise of instructions and scolding. For her, quietness is as precious as gold, and the freedom to act without constant supervision is sweet and refreshing.

#### Q4. Is Amanda an orphan? Why does she wish to be one?
**Answer:** No, Amanda is not an orphan; she lives with her parents. She wishes to be an orphan in her imagination because she believes it would grant her complete freedom to roam freely and quietly without anyone controlling or scolding her.

#### Q5. What is the irony in the last stanza of the poem?
**Answer:** The irony lies in the parent warning Amanda not to look moody or sulky so that outsiders don't think she is being nagged. In reality, the parent has been incessantly nagging her throughout the entire poem.
